How to choose a software development company in Malaysia: 12 questions to ask

Most software projects that go wrong were in trouble before a line of code was written. These questions help you choose a partner who will get the foundations right.

By Ritesh Parmar, Software Solution ArchitectPublished 15 Sep 2023
In short

Choose a software company that studies your workflow before quoting, shows similar work, delivers in phases with working demos, is clear about who owns the code, data and hosting, and explains what training and support look like after launch. Prepare your current forms, spreadsheets and a list of users before the first meeting, so every company is quoting the same problem.

Key takeaways
  1. Bring real forms and spreadsheets to the first meeting. They make quotations comparable.
  2. A fast quote with no questions asked is a warning sign, not a bonus.
  3. Ask about ownership, hosting and support before price, not after.
  4. Working demos during development beat a big reveal at the end.

Before the first meeting: what to prepare

You do not need a technical specification. You do need to show how your business works today, so that each company is quoting the same problem. Bring:

  • Sample documents: the forms, quotations, delivery orders, invoices and reports you use now.
  • The spreadsheets and chat groups that hold daily work, with a note on who updates each one.
  • A list of users and roles: who enters data, who approves, who only needs reports.
  • Your top three problems, in plain words. "We never know real stock" is perfect.
  • Must-haves versus nice-to-haves, a rough budget range and any real deadline.

Why these questions matter

Understanding your business

The first three questions reveal whether a company builds around your workflow or squeezes you into a template. A good partner asks a lot before quoting, and sometimes suggests a better way to structure the idea than the one you came in with.

How the project is delivered

Phases and working demos protect you. You see real screens early, your team gives feedback while changes are cheap, and nothing is a surprise on launch day. Testing with your real cases, not invented ones, is where most hidden problems surface.

Ownership and technology

Ask plainly who owns the source code and the data, where the system will be hosted and who pays for hosting. There is no single right answer, but there should be a clear one, in writing, before you sign. Ask why a particular technology is recommended; "because it fits your situation" should come with a reason you can understand.

After launch

Launch is the start of using the system, not the end of the project. Training, how you report issues, response expectations and how later changes are charged all decide whether the system is still useful in three years.

Red flags

  • A firm quote after a ten-minute call, with no questions about how you work.
  • A "custom" system that turns out to be a ready-made product with your logo on it.
  • No demos until the end of the project.
  • Vague answers on code ownership, data access or hosting.
  • No mention of training, support or what happens when something breaks.
  • Only one person in the company understands your project, and they are hard to reach.

Frequently asked questions

How many software companies should I talk to?

Two or three is usually enough. Give each one the same documents and problem list so their proposals are comparable, and pay attention to the questions they ask, not only the price.

Should I choose a software company near me?

Location matters less than how well they understand your business. Most of the work happens in shared meetings, demos and reviews, and the system itself is hosted in the cloud. Choose the team whose process and past work fit your needs.

Why do quotations for the same system vary so much?

Usually because each company has understood the scope differently. One may include data migration, training and support while another does not. Asking the same twelve questions makes those differences visible.
